# Working well as a remote team

A remote team mostly runs on writing things down and on everyone knowing which tasks are theirs. However, colleagues also need some reason to talk to each other, since there is no office where they would run into each other anyway.

Remote teams tend to get stuck when questions are saved for the next meeting. If you write the question down instead, the other person can answer it when they have time (and the answer is still there for the next person who asks).

Pick the channel by how soon you need an answer. Chat is for things that can't wait more than an hour, while email works for updates someone should read the same day, and video calls are worth it when the team disagrees or the problem is hard to explain in text. Anything you decide that should last longer than a week belongs in a shared document, because nobody will find it in the chat history later.

Counting hours only tells you when somebody was online. It works better to agree on the results each person will deliver and by when, and then check those, which of course only works if everyone can briefly say what they are working on this week.

Working from home can get lonely. A short weekly call with no agenda (or a chat channel for things that have nothing to do with work) gives the team a chance to talk, although nobody should have to join, since some will want it and some won't.

You will need a task tracker, video calls, shared documents and some way to see the time for each colleague. Which product you pick for each of these matters less than the whole team using the same one.

Time zones probably cause the most trouble. If your team spans more than a few hours, move the regular meeting around so that the same colleagues don't always get the late slot, and let most of the work happen without meetings at all.

Written messages also get misread. A short reply that sounds fine to you can sound curt to someone else, so if a message seems rude, assume it wasn't meant that way and ask before you react.

Remote workers also work too much when the office is the kitchen table. Agree on working hours, and don't message anyone who is on leave. If managers don't follow this themselves, nobody else will either.

Would any of this help your team? The easiest way to find out is to ask them every few months, and to watch whether projects ship on time and whether colleagues stay.
